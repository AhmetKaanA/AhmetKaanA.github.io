Ahmet Kaan Aydin

Ph.D. Candidate in Applied Mathematics · Scientific Computing

I am a Ph.D. Candidate in Applied Mathematics at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County (UMBC). My current research interests include numerical analysis, scientific computing, uncertainty quantification, low-rank methods based on tensor decomposition, and the observer/controller design for partial differential equations. I also build 3D phase-field simulations of collective cell migration, following border cells as they travel through the growing Drosophila egg chamber.

Research Highlights

border cells polar cells Optimal Transport for Border Cell Migration: Interactions Inside the Cluster

In the egg chamber of the fruit fly, a small cluster of border cells travels between large nurse cells to reach the oocyte, the future egg. We build 3D models of this journey in which the cells and the signal that guides them shape each other. The oocyte releases a chemical signal that spreads only through the narrow spaces between the cells, so the shapes of the cells decide the trail the cluster follows. The cluster, in turn, crawls along the nurse cells and pushes them aside, which reshapes the spaces the signal travels through. In our phase-field model, every cell is a soft body, and the egg chamber grows during the journey as it does in stage 9 of egg development, with the oocyte growing fastest. Our hybrid model keeps this soft chamber but builds the cluster from ten individual cells, using optimal transport to share the space among them: each cell keeps its size, no two cells overlap, and their shapes adapt on their own as the cluster squeezes through.

Flow speed Pressure Uncertainty Low-Rank Stochastic Navier–Stokes Solvers

The Navier–Stokes equations describe how fluids such as water and air move, but the properties of a real fluid are rarely known exactly. We simulate flow through a channel with a narrow section when the viscosity, the fluid's resistance to flowing, is uncertain. Rather than rerunning the simulation for many possible viscosities, our stochastic Galerkin solver computes how the flow depends on the uncertainty directly, solving all time steps together and storing the result in a compressed low-rank form that keeps the problem small enough to solve. In the video, the fluid starts from rest. On top, the average flow speeds up through the narrow section, with tracer particles following it. In the middle, the pressure pushes the fluid along the channel and drops most through the narrow section. At the bottom, the uncertainty in the velocity gathers just before, inside and just after the narrow section, where the flow changes fastest.

Beams and other flexible structures are often controlled with just one sensor and one actuator at the tip. To simulate them, a computer model cuts the beam into small pieces, and the standard ways of doing this add fast, artificial vibrations that the tip sensor can barely detect. A controller tested on such a model then looks weaker than it really is, and more so as the model gets finer. Our order-reduced finite differences (ORFD) build the right behavior into the model itself, so the sensor still sees every vibration the model can make. In the video, the same controller acts on both models: the unfiltered one (top) keeps shaking and its energy stays high, while the ORFD one (bottom) calms down as its energy drains away.

Total energy Sensor Actuator Piezoelectric Beam Stabilization

A magnetizable piezoelectric beam vibrates both mechanically and electrically. Ours is clamped at one end and controlled from the free end with two measurements, the tip velocity and the electric current, which are amplified and fed back to the beam. Picking the two amplifier gains by trial and error often gives slow damping, even when the energy does eventually die out. From a mathematical analysis, we derive a range for each gain; with both gains inside their ranges, the vibrations die out fastest. In the video, the controller damps the beam: the sensor and actuator dials show the feedback at work, the voltage dial shows the electrical oscillation, and the total energy drops to zero.
